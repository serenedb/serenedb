The block-overflow class: four one-row re-prices, one fix

Why a CHECKPOINT could write a column segment past its 256KB block, why it took four distinct defects to close, and why the fixed engine ends up faster than the broken one.

The contract

A segment must fit in one block, and only the compressor knows where segments end

Rows live in memory until CHECKPOINT. Then each column of each row group is handed, 2,048 rows at a time, to a compression function that accumulates rows into an in-memory state and must decide — before serializing anything — when to stop and flush a segment. That decision is the cut. Everything in this document is about making the cut fire at the right row.

The trap

Segment size is not row-additive: bitpacked regions are count × width

A dict_fsst segment is a dictionary of distinct values plus bitpacked metadata regions. Several regions are dict_count-wide or tuple_count-wide, and their width is derived from the data (bits(entry_count), bits(max_encoded_length), …). Two consequences:

  • While every row is unique, the selection-free layouts are legal — native FSST_ONLY, and cleaved FSST_PLUS when row-order prefixes pay. Both map row i directly to entry i; neither stores a row→entry buffer (both still store the dictionary itself).
  • One row that changes a width, or ends uniqueness, re-prices every row already in the segment.

The machine

Estimates decide when to measure; measurements decide cuts; flushes write what was measured

Measuring a candidate layout exactly (RefreshCleave) costs an LCP scan, a Cartesian-tree DP and a walk — too much per row. So the machine is layered: a per-row trigger, a cheap upper bound, the real measurement, and a certificate — the last state a measurement proved to fit, which overshoot recovery can rewind to. The fix's contribution is the boxed terms and the rule at the bottom.

The algorithm being sized

The cleave: shared prefixes become groups, chosen by a DP that now pays for its groups

The serenedb-only _PLUS modes compress across dictionary entries: sort them (or keep row order), compute adjacent LCPs of the FSST-encoded bytes, build the Cartesian tree of the LCP array, and for every subtree either take the whole range as one group sharing its minimum LCP, or split into the children. Since the fix, taking a group must beat splitting by at least GROUP_COST — a group that cannot pay for its own metadata never forms.

The subtlest one

3The rewind that restored the rows but not the decision

A certificate is a promise: “11,800 rows fit in 245,994 bytes.” The fix's insight is that the promise is only valid for the candidate layout it was measured under — and the old rewind restored the rows while keeping a poisoned mode decision.

Found by the corpus, older than the branch

4The never-encoded path: half a million rows, one new value, +36KB

A dictionary under 4KB of raw bytes never FSST-encodes, so its segment is guarded only by a byte trigger — and at a few bits of selection per row, such segments legitimately hold hundreds of thousands of rows. The suite was green; the real 564k-document corpus kept failing on 1-in-3 loads, because parallel scan order decides whether the (2k)th distinct value arrives late enough to arm it. This defect exists in every prior release.

Why the fixed engine is faster

Measure without building: the layout is materialized once, not per check

Profiling the fixed-but-slow interim version showed the truth: the exact measurement ran ~867 times per 3M-row column, and each run materialized up to two full candidate layouts — entry vectors, prefix tables, hundreds of KB of writes — of which the cut consumed one integer.
